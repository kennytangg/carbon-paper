\begin{abstract}
Satellite platforms promise to replace field inventories in forest carbon crediting, but their estimates are rarely tested blind against the standard-formula answer, and two practical problems, clouds and confidence, are rarely quantified. We separate the question into two isolated grids. The right grid computes field ground truth step by step to SNI 7724, IPCC, CDM, and Verra rules, with a Monte Carlo uncertainty budget, and is sealed; the left grid sees only plot coordinates and dates, reads time-matched Sentinel-1/2, Landsat, PALSAR, and terrain data, and estimates carbon blind. A hash-chained commit-reveal ledger proves that predictions were fixed first. On 476 stock plots, blind error was 45--49\% relative RMSE: about 30\% on 1\,ha plots, over 100\% on small Indonesian plots, with saturation near 300\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$. A plot-level climatology of 22{,}697 Sentinel-2 acquisitions shows that only 21.5\% see an Indonesian plot clear, yet a common scene-level filter discards 65\% of those; a radar-led model lost two points without optical data. Cloud-aware conformal quantile regression, calibrated on synthetic cloud, kept 90\% coverage under full cloud, while a metadata confidence score ranked errors in the wrong direction. Project intervals that ignore correlated map error covered 74\% of projects; as auxiliary data for field plots, the satellite halved the plots needed on large-plot datasets. For annual growth on 196 Bornean plots, no satellite estimate beat assuming no change. Satellites can map stock and save field plots; growth still needs remeasurement, and confidence must be calibrated, not graded.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Two Ways to Measure Forest Carbon}
\emph{Traditional MRV} (measurement, reporting, and verification) is field based. Crews lay out plots, measure each stem's diameter $D$ and height $H$, identify species to assign wood density $\rho$, and pass these inputs through a published allometric equation~\cite{chave2014improved}. Carbon follows from a fixed carbon fraction and the CO$_2$ to C mass ratio. Methodologies such as Verra VM0047 prescribe this chain~\cite{verra2025vm0047}; its output is deterministic and is the number on which credits are issued. Nobody can change the formula: it is the international standard. What is costly is the visit, especially in remote Indonesian peat swamps and hill forests.

\emph{System-based MRV} replaces or supplements the visit with satellites and software. Optical sensors observe canopy greenness, radar observes canopy structure and moisture, and spaceborne lidar measures height~\cite{dubayah2020gedi}. Global biomass maps exist~\cite{santoro2021global,spawn2020harmonized}, and commercial platforms, including our own NusaCarbon prototype, turn polygons into carbon numbers. The open question is how close such numbers are to what the field formula would have produced at the same place and time.

\subsection{Why the Comparison Is Hard to Do Honestly}
Three traps inflate reported accuracy. First, \emph{leakage}: if a model is trained or tuned on the plots it is scored on, or on plots next to them, the score measures memory rather than skill; spatially blocked validation of large-scale biomass maps reveals much lower skill than random validation~\cite{ploton2020spatial,roberts2017cv}. Second, \emph{temporal mismatch}: a plot measured in 2009 and an image from 2023 describe different forests. Third, \emph{scale}: a plot is a fraction of a hectare while satellite pixels range from 10\,m to 4.6\,km. A fair test must isolate the estimator from the answer, match time explicitly, and report how error changes with scale.

Our own platform illustrates the need. An audit of the NusaCarbon code base found that its forest and mangrove figures depended mostly on default constants (for example, a fixed sequestration rate and tree density bins from NDVI) rather than on satellite measurement, that clouds were screened only at scene level, that the reported confidence was a grade computed from image metadata, and that no estimate had been compared with field data. This paper builds the comparison that was missing, and tests the cloud handling and the confidence statement as carefully as the estimate itself.

\subsection{Two Isolated Grids}
We separate the problem into two grids (Fig.~\ref{fig:grids}). The \textbf{right grid} is fixed: published field plots, the standard formula, a carbon density in t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$. We never modify it. The \textbf{left grid} is free: any satellite data, any algorithm, any machine-learning method, subject to one rule: it never sees the right grid's outcomes for the plots it is scored on. A ledger enforces the rule and makes it auditable.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\textbf{RQ1 (blind accuracy; research methodology).} How far does a blind, satellite-only estimate of aboveground carbon stock deviate from standard-formula field ground truth, and how does the deviation depend on the temporal gap between field and satellite observations and on the area over which it is aggregated?

\textbf{RQ2 (closing the gap; AI).} Which learning strategy brings the satellite estimate closest to the field answer: off-the-shelf global products, a model trained only on spaceborne lidar, a model transferred from other regions, or a model calibrated with $k$ local field plots? Are its prediction intervals honest?

\textbf{RQ3 (clouds and confidence; AI and methodology).} How often do clouds hide an Indonesian plot from optical satellites, how much accuracy does that cost, and can the estimate and its prediction interval stay honest when they do? At the scale of a project, does the stated uncertainty protect against over-crediting, and how many field plots does a satellite save?

\textbf{RQ4 (sequestration).} Can the annual change in carbon stock, the quantity credits are issued on, be estimated blind from satellites, and how certain is the field answer itself?

\textbf{RQ5 (distributed systems).} Can time-matched extraction for hundreds of plots and sensors be sharded, balanced, and made fault tolerant with bit-identical outputs, and can a hash-chained ledger prove that the left grid was blind?

\subsection{Contributions}
(i) A blind, time-matched evaluation protocol with a commit-reveal ledger, reusable for any MRV estimator. (ii) A right grid built step by step to SNI 7724, IPCC, CDM, Verra, and Gold Standard rules, with a Monte Carlo uncertainty budget, on 476 stock plots (148 Indonesian plots recomputed tree by tree) and 223 day-dated repeat-census plots in Borneo. (iii) An estimator ladder from zero field data to $k$ local plots, with spatially blocked validation. (iv) The first plot-level cloud climatology for Indonesian forest plots that we are aware of, a cloud-robust estimator, and \emph{cloud-aware conformal} intervals calibrated on synthetic cloud. (v) Project-level uncertainty that accounts for spatially correlated map error, model-assisted estimators that quantify how many field plots a satellite saves, and an over-crediting test under the Verra deduction. (vi) A sharded, idempotent extraction runner with measured scaling and fault tolerance.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Allometry and Standards}
Pantropical allometry links $D$, $H$, and $\rho$ to aboveground biomass (AGB)~\cite{chave2014improved}; the BIOMASS package propagates its uncertainty~\cite{rejou2017biomass}. Verra VM0047 and the HCSA toolkit specify sampling, measurement thresholds, and plot designs~\cite{verra2025vm0047,rosoman2017hcsa}. Investigations of REDD+ crediting show that unverifiable baselines produce over-crediting~\cite{west2023redd}, which motivates auditable estimates.

\subsection{Remote Sensing of Biomass}
Optical reflectance saturates over dense canopies. C-band radar mostly sees the upper canopy; L-band radar penetrates further and tracks woody biomass up to moderate densities~\cite{mitchard2009using}, but its backscatter can \emph{decrease} in very dense forest~\cite{mermoz2015decrease}. Spaceborne lidar from GEDI samples canopy height in 25\,m footprints and is converted to biomass by footprint models calibrated on field data~\cite{dubayah2020gedi,duncanson2022gedi}. Global AGB maps combine these signals: ESA CCI Biomass~\cite{santoro2021global}, the 2010 harmonized map of Spawn \textit{et al.}~\cite{spawn2020harmonized}, and the annual Chloris product~\cite{chloris2022}. Canopy height alone predicts plot carbon well where lidar is available~\cite{asner2014mapping}.

\subsection{Machine Learning and Honest Validation}
Gradient-boosted trees are strong tabular regressors~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}; models fitted on log biomass need a retransformation correction~\cite{duan1983smearing}. Spatial autocorrelation makes random cross-validation optimistic; blocked or leave-region-out designs are required~\cite{roberts2017cv,ploton2020spatial}. Conformal prediction yields distribution-free intervals with finite-sample coverage~\cite{vovk2005algorithmic}. Agreement is assessed with RMSE, bias, and Lin's concordance~\cite{lin1989concordance}.

\subsection{Clouds and Uncertainty}
Southeast Asia is cloudy on at least 80\% of days~\cite{wilson2016cloud}, and scene metadata suggest at most one cloud-free Sentinel-2 scene per year over much of tropical Indonesia~\cite{sudmanns2020eocompass}. The combined Sentinel-2 and Landsat revisit is 2.9 days~\cite{li2017revisit}. Cloud masks such as Fmask and Cloud Score+ reach about 90\% accuracy, with thin cloud and shadow the main failures~\cite{zhu2012fmask,skakun2022cmix,pasquarella2023cloudscore}. Workarounds include best-available-pixel compositing~\cite{white2014pixel}, harmonized Landsat--Sentinel data~\cite{claverie2018hls}, SAR--optical fusion~\cite{reiche2016combining}, and deep SAR-to-optical cloud removal~\cite{meraner2020cloud}. On the uncertainty side, split conformal prediction~\cite{lei2018distribution}, conformalized quantile regression~\cite{romano2019cqr}, normalized~\cite{papadopoulos2008normalized} and Mondrian~\cite{bostrom2020mondrian} variants, and weighted or spatial conformal prediction under shift~\cite{tibshirani2019conformal,mao2024spatial} give distribution-free intervals. For biomass maps, the CEOS protocol~\cite{duncanson2021ceos} and Duncanson \textit{et al.}~\cite{duncanson2019importance} recommend plots of at least 1\,ha and explicit error propagation, and Araza \textit{et al.} separate map error from plot error~\cite{araza2022comprehensive}. Model-assisted estimators that combine remote sensing with field samples are standard in survey sampling~\cite{cochran1977sampling,sarndal1992model}.

\subsection{Integrity and Distributed Processing}
Hash-chained time-stamping makes a document's existence at a time verifiable~\cite{haber1991timestamp}; commit-reveal protocols bind a party to a value before another party reveals theirs~\cite{blum1983coin}. Large geospatial workloads are processed as independent map tasks~\cite{dean2008mapreduce}; consistent hashing places keys on workers with minimal remapping~\cite{karger1997consistent}, and Graham's longest-processing-time rule bounds makespan within $4/3$ of optimal~\cite{graham1969bounds}. Cloud catalogues such as the Microsoft Planetary Computer~\cite{mpc2022} and Google Earth Engine expose these archives through APIs.

\textbf{Step 1, tree.} Aboveground biomass of a tree with diameter $D$ (cm), height $H$ (m), and wood density $\rho$ (g\,cm$^{-3}$) follows Chave~2014 Eq.~4~\cite{chave2014improved},
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AGB}_i=0.0673\,(\rho_i D_i^2 H_i)^{0.976}\ \text{[kg]},\qquad \sigma_{\ln}=0.357,
\label{eq:chave4}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{\ln}$ is the residual standard error on the log scale. Where height is not measured, a local model $\ln H=a+b\ln D$ with Duan correction replaces it (SAFE: $a=0.549$, $b=0.656$, residual SD 0.348); Chave Eq.~7 with the environmental stress term $E$ is the fallback where no local heights exist. Wood density is the mean of the Global Wood Density Database~\cite{zanne2009gwdd,chave2009wood} at the finest level available, species, then genus, then family, preferring South-East Asian records. As a check on model choice we also apply the East-Kalimantan equation of Basuki \textit{et al.}~\cite{basuki2009allometric}, $\ln\mathrm{AGB}=-0.744+2.188\ln D+0.832\ln\rho$ (correction factor 1.047). Palms are excluded: Chave's equations are for dicotyledonous trees, and palm allometry depends on stem height, not diameter~\cite{goodman2013palm,lewis2020oilpalm}.

\textbf{Step 2, plot.} Tree biomass is expanded by the area in which a stem of its size is counted. For the HCSA nested design~\cite{rosoman2017hcsa},
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AGB}_j=\sum_{i\in j}\frac{\mathrm{AGB}_i}{1000\,a(D_i)},\quad a(D)=\begin{cases}0.01,&5\le D<15\\0.05,&D\ge 15\end{cases}
\label{eq:expand}
\end{equation}
in Mg\,ha$^{-1}$; SNI 7724 uses the same form, $C_n=C_x/1000\times10{,}000/l_\text{plot}$, with its own subplot areas~\cite{sni7724}.

\textbf{Step 3, pools and carbon.} The plot carbon density is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_j&=\mathrm{CF}\,(1+R)\,\mathrm{AGB}_j + C_{\mathrm{DW},j}+C_{\mathrm{L},j}+C_{\mathrm{SOC},j},\\
\mathrm{CO_2e}_j&=\tfrac{44}{12}\,C_j,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pools}
\end{equation}
with CF $=0.47$ (IPCC 2006 Table~4.3, unchanged in 2019; SNI 7724; Verra) or 0.475 (Gold Standard). For the root-to-shoot ratio $R$ the frameworks disagree: SNI 7724 Lampiran~A adopts the IPCC 2006 tropical-rainforest value $R=0.37$, while the 2019 Refinement gives 0.207 (SD 0.072) below 125\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ and 0.212 (SD 0.077) above for natural Asian tropical rainforest~\cite{ipcc2006v4,ipcc2019v4}. Dead wood, litter, and soil carbon are not in the public records; omitting a pool can only lower a stock claim, which is the conservative direction. All comparisons with the left grid use AGB, which removes the $R$ and CF choices from the benchmark; we report their effect separately.

\textbf{Step 4, stratum and project.} Following AR-TOOL14 Eqs.~14--17~\cite{artool14}, plots are grouped into strata $h$ (the HCS classes) with weights $w_h$, and the project mean and its relative uncertainty at 90\% are
\begin{equation}
\bar b=\sum_h w_h \bar b_h,\qquad u=\frac{t_{0.95,\,n-M}\sqrt{\sum_h w_h^2 s_h^2/n_h}}{\bar b},
\label{eq:strat}
\end{equation}
with $s_h^2$ the within-stratum variance, $n_h$ plots in stratum $h$, $n$ plots in total, and $M$ strata. The mapped stratum areas are not public, so the plot shares stand in for $w_h$. The number of plots needed for a margin $E$ (AR-TOOL03 Eq.~2, Neyman allocation, iterated on $t$ when $n<30$)~\cite{artool03} is
\begin{equation}
n=\left(\frac{t_{0.95}}{E}\right)^2\Big(\sum_h w_h s_h\Big)^2,\qquad n_h=n\,\frac{w_h s_h}{\sum_h w_h s_h}.
\label{eq:nplots}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 5, uncertainty.} AR-TOOL14 counts sampling error only. The IPCC asks for all sources, combined by Approach~1, $U=\sqrt{\sum_k U_k^2}$ for products and $U=\sqrt{\sum_k(U_kx_k)^2}/|\sum_k x_k|$ for sums, or by Monte Carlo (Approach~2)~\cite{ipcc2006v1}. We use Approach~2 with 2{,}000 draws per tree and the error models of the BIOMASS package~\cite{rejou2017biomass}: diameter error with SD $0.0062D+0.0904$\,cm for 95\% of stems and 4.64\,cm for a random 5\%~\cite{chave2004error}; height error of 10\%~\cite{chave2004error}; wood-density SD of 0.0708, 0.0941, and 0.1234\,g\,cm$^{-3}$ at species, genus, and family level; the allometric residual $\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_{\ln}^2)$; CF $\sim\mathcal N(0.4713,0.0206^2)$~\cite{thomas2012carbon}; and $R$ with its 2019 SD. The project error budget splits the variance of $\bar b$ into a sampling part (Eq.~\ref{eq:strat}) and a measurement-plus-allometry part (the Monte Carlo variance of the same weighted mean).

\textbf{Step 6, change and deduction.} Sequestration uses the IPCC stock-difference method, $\Delta C=(C_{t_2}-C_{t_1})/(t_2-t_1)$ (Vol.~4 Eq.~2.5), on the same plots at both dates~\cite{ipcc2006v4}. Its uncertainty follows VM0047 v1.1 Eq.~28~\cite{verra2025vm0047},
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
U&=\frac{T\sqrt{SE_0^2+SE_1^2-2\rho\,SE_0SE_1}}{\Delta C},\\
\mathrm{UNC}&=\min\big(1,\max(0,\ U-0.10)\big),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:vm0047}
\end{equation}
where $SE_0$ and $SE_1$ are the standard errors of the mean stock at the two dates, $\rho$ is their correlation on permanent plots, and $T$ is the two-tailed $t$ value at $\alpha=0.1$. Credits are multiplied by $1-\mathrm{UNC}$. For stocks, we report the deductions of VM0047 ($u-10\%$), AR-TOOL14 (a stepped share of $u$: 0 up to 10\%, then 25, 50, 75, 100\% of $u$ at 15, 20, 30\%), Gold Standard ($u-20\%$), and VMD0017 (95\% uncertainty minus 15\%)~\cite{artool14,gs2024ar,vmd0017}. In the Monte Carlo for change, a tree keeps the same allometric and height residual at both censuses, because the same stem is measured with the same equation; we also run the case in which they are redrawn.

\textbf{Data screening.} For HCSA, Step~1 is recomputed for every tree. Sixty-two records stated an area that contradicts Eq.~\ref{eq:expand} and were expanded by the protocol area. Whole plots were excluded, never imputed, if any record had a missing input, $H<1.3$\,m, conflicting coordinates, or $D>200$\,cm. The last rule caught plot RU09 (Riau), where a \textit{Gluta renghas} was recorded at $D=480$\,cm, which matches a circumference typed into the diameter column ($480/\pi\approx153$\,cm); left in place it would have produced 4{,}426\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$. Of 185 plots, 148 were retained; the 15 records labelled as oil palm all fell in excluded plots. GEO-TREES publishes plot AGB from forest networks~\cite{geotrees2024}; 154 plots with withheld AGB were dropped. Labri\`ere \textit{et al.} publish 1\,ha units with AGB from Chave Eq.~4 and local height-diameter models, plus 95\% credible intervals~\cite{labriere2018insitu}; we use the 1\,ha units only.

The outcome table is written to a sealed file. Only a manifest of plot id, region block, coordinates, date, date precision, and extraction radius is released to the left grid.

\subsection{Temporal Matching}
Field inventories record a date or, more often, a year; no public inventory records the minute of measurement, and optical satellites revisit every 5--16 days with frequent cloud. We therefore match by window: for a plot measured at $t_j$, the left grid uses only clear observations in $[t_j-W,\,t_j+W]$, records the offset in days of the closest one, and takes the median of up to three clear scenes. The default is $W=90$\,days; RQ1 varies $W\in\{30,90,180,365\}$ on the Indonesian plots. Each record also carries its timestamp precision (\emph{day}, \emph{year}, or \emph{campaign year}) so that the analysis can separate sensor noise from date uncertainty.

\subsection{Left Grid: Satellite Features}
For each plot the left grid reads, inside a square window around the plot centre: Sentinel-2 L2A reflectance in ten bands with scene-classification cloud masking and seven indices (NDVI, EVI, NDRE, NDMI, NBR, IRECI, SAVI); Landsat 5--9 Collection~2 surface reflectance with QA cloud masking; Sentinel-1 RTC backscatter (VV, VH, ratio); the ALOS PALSAR annual L-band mosaic (HH, HV); and Copernicus 30\,m elevation and slope. Two global products are read as zero-shot baselines: Chloris annual AGB (2003--2019)~\cite{chloris2022} and the 2010 harmonized map~\cite{spawn2020harmonized}. GEDI L4A footprint biomass and L2A relative height~\cite{duncanson2022gedi} are read through Earth Engine. That path is implemented, but a project-permission error on the Earth Engine account blocked it during this study, so no reported result uses GEDI.

\subsection{Clouds}
Southeast Asia is cloudy on at least 80\% of days, with no reliable clear season~\cite{wilson2016cloud}, and Sentinel-2 metadata for 2017 showed at most one cloud-free scene over much of tropical Indonesia~\cite{sudmanns2020eocompass}. We found no published clear-observation statistic for Kalimantan at plot scale, so we measured one, then designed the estimator so that clouds cost as little as possible.

\textbf{C1, plot-level climatology.} For 97 locations (one per 0.1$^\circ$ cell holding a plot), we read the Sentinel-2 scene classification (SCL) of every acquisition from 2021 to 2023 in a 5$\times$5 window of 20\,m pixels around the plot: 22{,}697 acquisitions after duplicates of the same date were merged. An acquisition is \emph{clear} if every pixel is vegetation, bare soil, water, or unclassified. From this we estimate the per-acquisition clear probability $p$, its monthly cycle, the gaps between clear looks, and the probability of at least one clear look within $\pm W$ days of a random date, which we compare with the independence model $1-(1-p)^m$ for $m$ acquisitions in the window; the difference measures how much clouds cluster in time. We also test the scene-level filter used by many Earth Engine pipelines, including our own platform (\texttt{CLOUDY\_PIXEL\_PERCENTAGE}~$<30$ or $<40$), against the plot condition.

\textbf{C2, missing optical data.} When clouds hide a plot, the optical block (Sentinel-2 and Landsat features) is missing. For a fraction $q\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ of each target region's plots we remove it and compare four strategies, all leave-one-region-out: \emph{naive} (the full model, missing values routed down LightGBM's default branches); \emph{dropout}, one model trained on the real rows plus copies with the optical block removed, the modality-dropout idea of Neverova \textit{et al.}~\cite{neverova2016moddrop}; \emph{switch}, a cloud-aware ensemble that uses a radar-and-terrain model when optical data are missing; and \emph{impute}, optical features regressed from radar, a simple form of SAR-to-optical gap filling~\cite{meraner2020cloud}.

\textbf{Cloud-aware conformal intervals.} A prediction interval is only honest if it is calibrated on cases like the one being predicted. When clouds are rare in the calibration data, a group-conditional (Mondrian) calibration~\cite{bostrom2020mondrian} of the cloud-hit group has too few scores. We therefore calibrate on \emph{synthetic cloud}: every inner out-of-fold calibration plot is scored twice, as observed and with its optical block removed, with quantile models trained under modality dropout. The cloud-hit group then has as many calibration scores as the clear group. We call this cloud-aware conformalized quantile regression and test it on every target plot, both as observed and fully clouded.

\subsection{Estimator Ladder}
\textbf{E0 (off the shelf).} Global products read at the plot, no fitting. \textbf{E1 (no field data).} LightGBM trained only on GEDI footprint biomass labels sampled around each region, using the same satellite predictors; no field plot enters training (implemented, not yet evaluated). \textbf{E2 (transfer).} LightGBM on $\ln\mathrm{AGB}$, trained on all region blocks except the target block and predicting it blind (leave-one-region-out), with Duan smearing~\cite{duan1983smearing}. \textbf{E3 ($k$-shot).} E2 followed by a ratio calibration on $k$ randomly drawn plots of the target block, scored on its remaining plots, repeated 200 times per $k$. Split-conformal intervals for E2 are calibrated on inner leave-one-region-out residuals of the training blocks only~\cite{vovk2005algorithmic}.

\subsection{Confidence: Plot and Project Intervals}
\textbf{Plot intervals.} All intervals target 90\% and are calibrated on inner leave-one-region-out residuals of the training regions only. With calibration scores $s_1,\dots,s_n$, the conformal threshold is the $\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$-th smallest score, which guarantees marginal coverage between $1-\alpha$ and $1-\alpha+1/(n+1)$ under exchangeability~\cite{lei2018distribution}. We compare four scores on $y=\ln\mathrm{AGB}$:
\begin{align}
\text{split:}\quad & s=|y-\hat y|, \qquad \hat C=\hat y\pm q,\\
\text{Mondrian:}\quad & \text{split, one } q_g \text{ per availability group } g,\\
\text{normalized:}\quad & s=|y-\hat y|/\hat\sigma(x), \qquad \hat C=\hat y\pm q\,\hat\sigma(x),\\
\text{CQR:}\quad & s=\max\{\hat q_{\mathrm{lo}}(x)-y,\ y-\hat q_{\mathrm{hi}}(x)\},\\
& \hat C=[\hat q_{\mathrm{lo}}(x)-q,\ \hat q_{\mathrm{hi}}(x)+q],
\end{align}
following Papadopoulos \textit{et al.}~\cite{papadopoulos2008normalized} for the normalized score, with $\hat\sigma(x)$ a second LightGBM fitted to the inner residuals, and Romano \textit{et al.}~\cite{romano2019cqr} for conformalized quantile regression (CQR), with LightGBM quantile models at 5\% and 95\%. We report coverage, the median ratio of the upper to the lower bound, and the Winkler interval score on the log scale, $(\hat u-\hat l)+\tfrac{2}{\alpha}\big[(\hat l-y)_+ + (y-\hat u)_+\big]$. As Araza \textit{et al.} note, an interval calibrated on plots covers the plot value, which includes field error, not the true stock~\cite{araza2022comprehensive}.

\textbf{Is a metadata ``confidence score'' a confidence?} Monitoring platforms often grade their output with a score built from cloud cover, data completeness, and temporal offset. Ours did: 40 points for cloud cover, plus weights for completeness and stability. We rebuild such a score from the same inputs (40 points for clear optical scenes, 30 for the share of sensor groups present, 30 for temporal closeness) and test whether it ranks plots by their actual error, using Spearman's $\rho$ against $|\ln y-\ln\hat y|$, next to the learned $\hat\sigma(x)$.

\textbf{Project intervals.} Every region block with at least eight plots plays a project, whose true stock is the mean field AGB of its plots. For satellite-only estimates of the mean, a naive interval treats plot errors as independent, $SE=s_w/\sqrt n$. Map errors, however, are spatially correlated over 50--100\,km~\cite{araza2022comprehensive}, so a project shares one regional bias. The two-component interval adds the between-region variance of the region-mean log residual,
\begin{equation}
SE^2=\frac{s_w^2}{n}+s_b^2,
\label{eq:twocomp}
\end{equation}
with $s_w$ and $s_b$ estimated from the other regions only. When $k$ field plots are available, the satellite becomes auxiliary information for a model-assisted estimator~\cite{cochran1977sampling,sarndal1992model}. The ratio estimator is
\begin{equation}
\hat{\bar Y}_R=\frac{\sum_{i\in s}y_i}{\sum_{i\in s}\hat y_i}\,\bar{\hat y},\qquad \widehat{V}=\Big(1-\frac kn\Big)\frac{1}{k}\,s_e^2,\quad e_i=y_i-\hat R\hat y_i,
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\hat y}$ is the satellite mean over the whole project. The regression (GREG) estimator is $\bar y_s+\hat\beta(\bar{\hat y}-\bar{\hat y}_s)$. Both are compared with the field-only mean of the same $k$ plots over 500 random draws. The relative efficiency $\mathrm{MSE}_\text{field}/\mathrm{MSE}_\text{estimator}$ says how many field plots one satellite-assisted plot is worth. We use the empirical MSE over draws, because the GREG variance formula underestimates at small $k$. A conservative estimate $\hat Y(1-\mathrm{UNC})$ with the VM0047 rule exceeds the truth whenever the project is over-credited; we report how often.

\subsection{Scoring}
We report RMSE, relative RMSE, bias, $R^2$, and Lin's concordance~\cite{lin1989concordance} per dataset and overall, interval coverage for E2, error by field-AGB range, error versus window $W$, and the error of the mean of $n$ plots as $n$ grows, which approximates estimating a larger area.

\subsection{Sequestration}
Credits are issued on change, not stock. The only public tropical dataset we found that gives repeated per-stem measurements with \emph{day-precision} dates is the SAFE Project tree census in Sabah, Malaysian Borneo~\cite{svatek2025safe,ewers2011safe}: 25\,m$\times$25\,m plots across old-growth, logged, riparian, and oil-palm land, censused from 2010 to 2020, with every stem's measurement date recorded. It is also the closest field site to Kalimantan in our benchmark.

The right grid applies the same chain to every census: Chave Eq.~4 on stems with $D\geq10$\,cm; height from a local $\ln H=a+b\ln D$ model fitted to the heights SAFE measured, with Duan correction; wood density from the Global Wood Density Database~\cite{zanne2009gwdd,chave2009wood} by species, then genus, then family, then plot mean, preferring South-East Asian records. For each plot with censuses at least three years apart, sequestration is
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathrm{AGB}_j=\frac{\mathrm{AGB}_j(t_1)-\mathrm{AGB}_j(t_0)}{t_1-t_0}\ \ [\mathrm{Mg\,ha^{-1}\,yr^{-1}}],
\end{equation}
where $t_0$ and $t_1$ are the median stem dates of the first and last census. This gives 223 plots in 18 blocks, with $t_0$ between 2010 and 2015 and $t_1$ between 2016 and 2020. The manifest released to the left grid holds only plot id, block, coordinates, and the two dates.

The left grid estimates $\Delta\mathrm{AGB}$ three ways: \emph{no change} ($\Delta=0$, the baseline any estimator must beat); \emph{S0}, the difference of the Chloris annual maps between the census years; and \emph{S2}, the difference of two E2 stock predictions at $t_0$ and $t_1$, with E2 trained on the 476 stock plots only. Sentinel-2 did not exist at most $t_0$ dates, so S2 uses Landsat, Sentinel-1, PALSAR, and terrain. No SAFE label is released to any estimator; the same E2 model also predicts SAFE stock at $t_0$, which is an out-of-region test on Borneo.

\subsection{Blindness Ledger}
Every protocol step is an entry in an append-only log whose entries carry the SHA-256 of the previous entry~\cite{haber1991timestamp}. An evaluation epoch is: \texttt{seal} (digest of the sealed outcomes and of the public manifest), \texttt{commit} (digest of each estimator's prediction file), \texttt{release} (for E2, the digest and size of each fold's training labels, with a flag stating whether any target-block label was included), and \texttt{reveal} (accepted only if the sealed file still matches its digest). Commits after a reveal are refused. Editing any entry breaks every later hash, so a third party can verify the order of events from the log alone~\cite{blum1983coin}.

\subsection{Distributed Extraction}
One task is one (plot, date, window, sensor) request. Its result is stored under the SHA-256 of the canonical task, which makes re-execution idempotent and turns the cache into a checkpoint: a restarted run skips every finished task. Writes are atomic (write then rename). Transient errors are retried with capped exponential backoff and jitter. Tasks are grouped into spatial tiles so a worker reuses scenes for neighbouring plots, but field plots cluster (one site holds 89 plots), so a tile holding more than the per-worker mean is split into sub-buckets by plot id, keeping all sensors of a plot together. Buckets are then placed either by consistent hashing~\cite{karger1997consistent} or by the longest-processing-time rule~\cite{graham1969bounds}. On the 2{,}052 tasks of this study with 24 workers, the worst-worker load factor $\lambda=\max_w L_w/\bar L$ was 6.75 for tile-only placement, 2.18 for split tiles with consistent hashing, and 1.40 for split tiles with LPT.

\section{Results}

\subsection{How Certain Is the Right Grid?}
Before scoring the satellites we score the field answer.

\textbf{Plot level.} On the 148 HCSA plots, the Monte Carlo 95\% interval of plot AGB has a median half-width of 31\% (IQR 25--45\%). Of the stems, 45\% have a species-level wood density, 42\% genus-level, 7\% family-level, and 5\% none. The Monte Carlo median is 5\% above the deterministic value, because the allometric error is log-normal; the deterministic value is the one the standards use.

\textbf{Equation choice.} The regional equation of Basuki \textit{et al.} gives almost the same total over all plots (ratio 1.01). By region, however, the stock moves from $-40$\% (Central Kalimantan) to $+121$\% (South Sumatra), because it ignores height where HCSA measured short trees.

\textbf{Pool choice.} The root-to-shoot ratio of SNI 7724 (IPCC 2006) gives 13\% more total carbon than the 2019 Refinement. Two standards, both valid, differ by more than the precision target of most methodologies.

\textbf{Project level.} Table~\ref{tab:gtproj} applies AR-TOOL14 to each province. The sampling uncertainty at 90\% is 9--34\%, and five of six provinces meet SNI 7724's 20\% limit. But measurement and allometric error, which AR-TOOL14 does not count, carry 10--64\% of the variance of the project mean. Their share is largest where the plots vary least between each other (Riau, 64\%). The deductions differ by standard for the same data (Jambi: 24\% under VM0047, 34\% under AR-TOOL14, 14\% under Gold Standard). Reaching $\pm$10\% at 90\% would take 8 to 71 plots per province.

\textbf{Change.} Growth is far harder than stock. On SAFE, the Monte Carlo SD of a plot's $\Delta$AGB is 2.0\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ when a tree keeps its allometric residual across censuses, against 4.3 when it is redrawn. The signal itself is 1.9. Remeasuring the same stems with the same equation cancels half the error. At block level, Eq.~\ref{eq:vm0047} gives 90\% uncertainties of 101--901\% when the correlation of the two stock means is ignored ($\rho=0$), which is a 100\% deduction for 13 of 15 blocks and over 90\% for the other two. With the measured $\rho$ (0.45--0.99), the uncertainty falls to 28--469\%, and seven blocks keep some credit, the best at an 18\% deduction. With 9--16 plots of 0.06\,ha per block, even the field standard cannot certify growth of 2\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$, unless it uses the correlation that permanent plots provide.

\subsection{Blind Accuracy of Carbon Stock (RQ1)}
Table~\ref{tab:stock} gives the blind scores. All three estimators were committed to the ledger before the reveal. Over all 476 plots the best blind estimate, E2, misses the field value by a relative RMSE of 48.5\% and explains 31\% of its variance; the Chloris product, which was never fitted to any of our plots, is almost as close (49.3\%). The 2010 harmonized map is worse (57.5\%, $R^2=0.03$), partly because it describes 2010 while most plots were measured years later. Because carbon is a fixed multiple of AGB on both grids, every relative figure applies unchanged to t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$ and t\,CO$_2$e\,ha$^{-1}$.

The dataset matters more than the estimator. On the 1\,ha Labri\`ere units E2 is within 28.6\% (CCC 0.55); on GEO-TREES within 41.9\%; on the Indonesian HCSA plots every estimator misses by more than 100\% and none beats predicting the mean ($R^2<0$).

\textbf{Saturation.} Fig.~\ref{fig:scatter} shows the pattern. Binned by field AGB, both E0 and E2 regress toward $\sim$300\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$. For plots above 400\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ (mean 480) the E2 mean prediction is 293 and the Chloris mean is 295, a median under-estimate of about one third. Below 50\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ (mean 21) E2 predicts 60 and Chloris 178. Between 100 and 300\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ the median error of E2 is only $+5$ to $+12$\%. The satellite signal therefore carries useful information in the middle of the range and little at either end, which is the behaviour reported for optical and L-band signals~\cite{mitchard2009using,mermoz2015decrease}.

\textbf{Scale.} The HCSA plots are nested 0.01/0.05\,ha plots with a median of 15 stems, so one large tree more or less moves the plot by hundreds of Mg\,ha$^{-1}$. Their field values span 2 to 876\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ within one province. A 30\,m pixel window cannot resolve that, and the 4.6\,km Chloris pixel averages open land and forest together, which explains its large over-estimate on HCSA open-land and scrub plots. The HCSA land-cover labels would separate these plots well (within-class standard deviations of 10--40\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ for all classes except high-density forest), but they are released with the field records, so they belong to the right grid and are never shown to the left grid. A satellite classifier that reproduced the HCS stratification is a promising left-grid estimator for future work.

\textbf{Field truth is not the bottleneck.} Labri\`ere publish a 95\% credible interval for every unit; the implied standard error of the field value is 5.0\% (RMS), against a 28.6\% satellite error on the same units. The right grid is far more precise than the left grid, so the gap is a satellite gap, not a ground-truth gap.

\textbf{Time.} Re-extracting the Indonesian plots' optical and C-band features with $W=30$, 90, 180, and 365 days changed their blind error by less than three points (rRMSE 102.5, 104.8, 102.7, 102.0\%), while the share of plots with at least one clear Sentinel-2 scene rose from 74\% at 30 days to 92\% at 90 and 100\% at 180. A tighter window costs coverage in the tropics and buys no accuracy when the field date is only known to the year, and when plot-scale noise dwarfs seasonal change. Temporal matching matters where the forest changes between the two dates, which is the sequestration case below.

\subsection{Closing the Gap (RQ2)}
\textbf{Better models.} Table~\ref{tab:ladder} adds one information source at a time, always leave-one-region-out. These variants were run after the reveal, so they are exploratory; every variant we tried is reported. Radar alone is ahead of optical alone (54.5\% vs 56.9\% overall; 33.7\% vs 42.0\% on the 1\,ha units), the two together beat either, terrain helps further, and feeding the Chloris product to the model as one more feature helps again. Of these steps, only the last one is clearly outside sampling noise (Section~\ref{sec:reliability}): the simple average of E2 and Chloris is the best blind estimator overall (45.4\%, $R^2=0.39$), 3.7 points better than E2 with a region-bootstrap 95\% interval of $-7.2$ to $-0.6$. None of these steps rescues the HCSA plots.

\subsection{Clouds and Confidence (RQ3)}

\textbf{The scene filter is the real problem.} Scene-level cloud percentage agrees only loosely with the plot (Spearman $-0.67$). In Indonesia, the rule \texttt{CLOUDY\_PIXEL\_PERCENTAGE}~$<30$ keeps 11.5\% of acquisitions, but 34\% of the scenes it keeps are cloudy over the plot, and it throws away 65\% of the acquisitions in which the plot is clear. At $<40$, the threshold our own platform uses for segmentation, 40\% of the kept scenes are cloudy over the plot. Masking at the plot instead of filtering scenes gives 2.8 times as many usable clear looks (21.5\% against 7.6\% of acquisitions) and removes the cloudy ones that pass the filter.

\textbf{What clouds cost the estimate.} Table~\ref{tab:cloudrob} removes the optical block from a growing share of target plots. \textbf{A metadata score is not a confidence.} The rebuilt platform-style confidence score correlates with the actual error in the \emph{wrong} direction over all plots (Spearman $+0.39$): plots scored as most reliable were the least accurate. The reason is that the recent, cloud-checked HCSA plots score high and are the hardest to predict. Within datasets the correlation is weak ($+0.06$, $-0.18$, $+0.17$). The learned difficulty $\hat\sigma(x)$ ranks errors correctly ($+0.41$ overall; $+0.29$, $+0.12$, $+0.08$ within datasets). Image quality is a property of the input. Confidence is a property of the error, and it has to be calibrated against field data.

\textbf{Project-level confidence and over-crediting.} For 19 projects (blocks with at least 8 plots), the blind satellite mean missed the field mean by a median of 23\%. The naive interval, with plot errors assumed independent, had a median half-width of 28\% but covered only 74\% of projects (HCSA 33\%), and two projects would have been over-credited even after the VM0047 deduction. The two-component interval of Eq.~\ref{eq:twocomp} covered all 19, at an honest half-width of 71--86\%, which under VM0047 means 61--76\% of the credits are deducted. A satellite-only project estimate is therefore either over-confident or almost uncreditable.

Field plots change this (Fig.~\ref{fig:modelassist}, Table~\ref{tab:modelassist}). With the satellite as auxiliary data, the ratio estimator is 1.4 to 2.3 times as efficient as the field-only mean on GEO-TREES ($k=3$ to 20) and about 2 times on Labri\`ere ($k=3$ to 10); at $k=20$ the small Labri\`ere regions are nearly fully sampled and the advantage disappears. Equivalently, the satellite replaces roughly half of the field plots needed for a given precision: summed over regions, the plots needed for $\pm$10\% at 90\% fall from 408 to 216 (GEO-TREES) and from 381 to 162 (Labri\`ere). On HCSA, where the satellite cannot rank small plots (within-region correlation 0.2), the ratio estimator is less efficient than field-only at every $k$ (0.54--0.96), only GREG gains at $k=20$ (1.25), and the plots needed rise from 1{,}483 to 1{,}877. GREG under-states its own variance at small $k$ (coverage 66--73\% at $k=3$), so the ratio estimator is the safer choice. Finally, the VM0047 deduction does not make an estimate conservative: with $k=10$ plots, the deducted estimate still exceeded the true stock in 32--43\% of draws on the large-plot datasets, for all three estimators, because the deduction only starts above 10\% uncertainty. The lower end of a 90\% interval, by construction, exceeds the truth in about 5\% of draws.

\subsection{Sequestration (RQ4)}
\textbf{A right-grid failure first.} After the reveal, the 27 oil-palm plots showed a mean field stock of 505\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$, more than old-growth forest. Every stem in those plots is an oil palm whose recorded ``diameter'' (median 76\,cm) includes the frond bases, and Chave Eq.~4 is a model for dicotyledonous trees. The standard formula is therefore not valid there. The evaluation script had fixed an ``excluding oil palm'' subset before the reveal, and we report it as the primary result: 196 plots in 15 blocks, mean field $\Delta\mathrm{AGB}=1.87$\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ (0.88\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$), median census gap 8.4 years.

\textbf{Blind satellite change estimates do not beat ``no change''.} Table~\ref{tab:seq} gives the scores. At plot level the Chloris difference (S0) has almost the same error as predicting zero (RMSE 8.06 vs 8.12\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$, $r=0.07$). The E2 stock difference (S2) is twice as bad and has the wrong sign: it predicts a loss of 4\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ where the forest gained 1.9. The cause is a sensor artefact: Sentinel-1 existed for only 13\% of the plots at $t_0$ but for all of them at $t_1$, and no L-band mosaic before 2015 is available in the catalogue, so the two stock predictions are made from different inputs. Restricting the stock model to sensors present at both dates (S2c: Landsat and terrain) halves the bias but not the noise.

\textbf{Why.} The blind E2 stock error on these plots has a standard deviation of 146\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$. If the errors at $t_0$ and $t_1$ were independent, differencing them over 8.4 years would add noise of about 25\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$, thirteen times the signal. The errors are partly correlated, because the same place is seen twice, which is why the observed S2c error (14\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$) is lower, but it is still seven times the signal. A stock estimator with plot-scale error of 30--100\% cannot resolve growth of 1\% per year.

\textbf{What does work is not a satellite.} Two estimators trained on the other SAFE blocks' measured change (leave-one-block-out, run after the reveal and therefore exploratory) were added: a LightGBM on Landsat and Chloris change features (S3), and the plain mean rate of the other blocks with no satellite input at all. The regional default rate is the most accurate estimator at both scales (block-mean MAE 0.98 vs 2.05 for no change), and S3 learns little more than that rate. At this plot size and with these sensors, the information about growth comes from nearby field remeasurements, not from the pixels.

\subsection{Systems (RQ5)}
\textbf{Production runs.} The stock extraction ran 2{,}052 tasks on 24 workers in 2.1\,h (0.26 tasks\,s$^{-1}$) with a measured load factor of 1.47, close to the 1.40 predicted from task counts; 85 transient errors were absorbed by retries. We stopped the first attempt, which used tile-only placement, to switch policies; the restart skipped the 92 tasks already in the content-addressed cache. Seventeen tasks (0.8\%) failed permanently: 13 hit two Landsat scenes whose files could not be read from the catalogue, and four hit unreadable Sentinel-2 files. Retrying cannot repair a permanently broken input; the runner records such tasks and the model treats their features as missing, and a scene-level fallback is the obvious next step. The SAFE extraction ran 2{,}006 tasks on 16 workers in 2.3\,h with no permanent failures, 36 retries, and a load factor of 1.44.

\textbf{Scaling, faults, and resume.} A random sample of 48 tasks (Landsat, Sentinel-1, PALSAR, terrain) was run on 1 to 32 workers, each time on an empty cache (Table~\ref{tab:sys}). Throughput rose 5.1-fold up to 8 workers and then flattened (5.5-fold at 32). Two limits explain this. With 48 tasks, 32 workers hold one or two buckets each, so the load factor rose to 2.67. And the makespan cannot fall below the slowest single task, a multi-scene read, however many workers wait beside it. The extraction is bound by remote reads, not by local compute. Every run was compared with the single-worker run task by task: all 47 finished results were bit-identical at every worker count. With transient faults injected at rates 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 per attempt, the runner absorbed 13, 18, and 28 retries, and again all 47 results matched. The one task that never finished, in any run, is the one whose Landsat file is unreadable, which is a permanent fault. In the resume test, a run stopped after 23 tasks; the restart skipped those 23 from the cache, ran the remaining 24, and produced output identical to the uninterrupted run.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Can Satellites Replace the Field Plot?}
Not at the scale of a plot, and not for growth. Tested blind, open satellite data put a 1\,ha plot's carbon stock within roughly 30--40\% of the field-formula value, and a 0.05\,ha plot within about 100\%; the best honest interval (CQR) is a factor of five wide. For sequestration, the quantity on which credits are paid, no satellite estimator we built beat assuming no change, and a regional average of measured growth beat all of them. A platform that issues plot-level or annual growth numbers from these sensors alone is reporting a default constant, whether or not it says so.

What satellites do well is replace part of the inventory. Used as auxiliary data in a ratio estimator, they halved the number of field plots needed for a given precision on large-plot datasets (Table~\ref{tab:modelassist}). The useful framing is not ``satellites instead of field plots'' but ``satellites plus a small, well-designed set of field plots instead of a full inventory''. Even that only holds where the satellite can rank plots within a project. On the small Indonesian plots it cannot (within-region correlation 0.2), and there it does not save a single plot.

\subsection{Our Numbers Against the Literature}
Table~\ref{tab:bench} places our blind results next to published validations. Our 1\,ha error (29\%) is close to the 31--38\% reported for Sentinel-1 and PALSAR in Kalimantan~\cite{berninger2018sar}. That study was validated against a lidar-upscaled reference layer, not blind against field plots, and it reports the same underestimation above 200\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$. Our saturation near 300\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ matches the flattening near 250\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ in the global map of Santoro \textit{et al.}~\cite{santoro2021global} and the steep rise of map error above 300\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ found by Araza \textit{et al.}~\cite{araza2022comprehensive}. Airborne lidar in Sabah, the best remote sensing can do, still errs by 25\% at 0.1\,ha and 13\% at 1\,ha~\cite{jucker2018estimating}. Plot size dominates every comparison. Local variation of AGB alone has a coefficient of variation of 46\% at 0.1\,ha and 17\% at 1\,ha~\cite{rejou2014local}, and Duncanson \textit{et al.} recommend plots of at least 1\,ha for validation~\cite{duncanson2019importance}. If we remove that expected representativeness noise in quadrature, the blind satellite error is about 23\% on the 1\,ha units and still about 90\% on HCSA. Plot size alone does not explain the Indonesian result. The mosaic of open land, scrub, and forest around HCSA plots, and a GPS error comparable to the 12.6\,m plot radius~\cite{duncanson2019importance}, are the likely remainder.

\subsection{Clouds: What Actually Matters}
Three findings change how clouds should be handled. First, \emph{how often} a plot is clear matters less than \emph{how} clearness is decided. A clear look within $\pm$30 days exists for 96\% of dates in Indonesia, because revisit compensates for an 80\% cloud frequency~\cite{wilson2016cloud,li2017revisit}. The scene-level filter that most pipelines apply, by contrast, discards two thirds of the clear looks and keeps a third of cloudy ones. Pixel-level masking at the plot, with SCL, Fmask, or Cloud Score+~\cite{zhu2012fmask,skakun2022cmix,pasquarella2023cloudscore}, nearly triples the usable data. Second, \emph{season matters}: in Indonesia the clear share in May is 4.4 times that in January, so field campaigns and their satellite windows should be planned for the dry months. Third, a model led by radar and an annual product loses only two points of accuracy without any optical data, so clouds are an availability problem for optical indices but not for a well-designed multi-sensor stock estimate. That is the practical form of the SAR--optical fusion argued for by Reiche \textit{et al.}~\cite{reiche2016combining}. Filling optical gaps from radar and feeding them to the model, however, was the worst option we tested. Reconstructed reflectance is a model output whose error does not disappear because the image looks complete.

\subsection{Confidence That Means Something}
Honest confidence has three layers, and each failed in a characteristic way. At \emph{plot} level, conformal intervals were honest overall, but only CQR was both honest and usable, and the cloud-aware calibration kept it honest when optical data were missing. The guarantee is marginal: under the shift from large to small plots (HCSA), coverage fell to 68--81\% for every method. Spatially weighted conformal prediction~\cite{tibshirani2019conformal,mao2024spatial} is the next step. At \emph{project} level, independence is the dangerous assumption. Map errors are correlated over tens of kilometres~\cite{araza2022comprehensive}, a project shares one regional bias, and an interval that ignores it covered only 74\% of projects. The two-component interval is honest, and it shows that a satellite-only project estimate would lose most of its credits under VM0047. At the level of the \emph{standard}, a deduction of $U-10\%$ is not a lower confidence bound. With ten field plots, the deducted estimate still exceeded the true stock in a third of draws. Crediting the lower bound of a calibrated interval instead would make the over-crediting rate a design parameter rather than an accident.

A metadata quality score answers a different question from a confidence interval. Ours rewarded recent, cloud-checked imagery, and the plots it trusted most were the plots the model got most wrong. Confidence has to be learned from errors against field data and reported in the units of the claim, as an interval in t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$, not as a grade.

\subsection{The Ground Truth Is a Model Too}
The right grid is fixed by the standards, but the standards leave real choices, and those choices are as large as the precision targets they enforce. Switching the root-to-shoot ratio from the SNI 7724 value (IPCC 2006) to the 2019 Refinement changes total carbon by 13\%. Switching from the pantropical Chave equation to the East-Kalimantan equation of Basuki \textit{et al.} leaves the national total unchanged but moves provincial stocks from $-40$\% to $+121$\%. AR-TOOL14 counts sampling error only, yet measurement and allometry carried up to 64\% of the variance of a provincial mean. The same data receive deductions from 0 to 34\% depending on which standard is applied. For growth the field answer itself is fragile. With 9--16 plots of 0.06\,ha, VM0047 Eq.~28 deducts most or all of the credit unless the correlation of permanent plots is used, and the plot-level measurement error of the change halves only when the same stem is assessed with the same equation at both dates. The target ``100\% accuracy'' therefore has no meaning. The meaningful target is to fall within the uncertainty of a field estimate made to a stated standard, and that uncertainty must itself be computed in full.

\subsection{The Path Toward the Field Answer}
The experiments rank the levers. (i) \emph{Report at project scale}: aggregation removes most random error. (ii) \emph{Add local plots and use the satellite as auxiliary data}, which is the most effective step, provided the plots are large enough to be ranked by the satellite. (iii) \emph{Mask clouds at the plot, not the scene}, and match the window to the season. (iv) \emph{Lead with structure-sensitive sensors}. Radar was ahead of optical on both large-plot datasets, though within sampling noise, and it is what keeps the estimate alive under cloud; spaceborne lidar (GEDI) and the 2025 L- and P-band missions NISAR and ESA BIOMASS are the next steps; our GEDI-only estimator E1 is implemented but was blocked by an access-permission issue during this study. (v) \emph{Keep sensors consistent through time}: half of the sequestration error came from Sentinel-1 appearing between the two censuses. (vi) \emph{Combine rather than choose}: averaging the transfer model with an independent global product was the best blind stock estimate. (vii) \emph{Calibrate confidence}: CQR with cloud-aware calibration at plot level, the two-component variance at project level, and credit the lower bound.

\subsection{Implications for NusaCarbon}
Our audit found default constants where measurements were expected, a scene-level cloud filter, and a confidence grade computed from image metadata. The evidence suggests a concrete redesign:
\begin{itemize}
\item Mask clouds per pixel at the plot (SCL or Cloud Score+) instead of \texttt{CLOUDY\_PIXEL\_PERCENTAGE}, and widen the window in the wet season.
\item Estimate stock with a radar-led multi-sensor model trained with modality dropout, and attach a cloud-aware CQR interval instead of the letter grade.
\item Report projects with the two-component interval, and calibrate each project with ten to twenty large field plots through a ratio estimator.
\item Credit the lower confidence bound.
\item Credit sequestration from field remeasurement of permanent plots, and use satellites to detect loss and disturbance.
\item Log every estimate through the commit-reveal ledger.
\item Fix the GEDI band that is read as canopy height.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\emph{Internal.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The right grid is itself a model: Chave allometry carries its own error, and GEO-TREES and Labri\`ere AGB were computed by their authors, not by us.
\item HCSA is released as a 2023 dataset without per-plot dates, and Labri\`ere plots have campaign-year dates only, so temporal matching for those plots is at year precision.
\item The standard formula failed on SAFE oil palm. Those plots are excluded by a subset fixed before the reveal, and 45 palm stems remain in forest plots.
\item The improvement ladder, the change models, the cloud and interval experiments, and the project-level analysis were defined after the reveals and are exploratory. Only the E0, E2, S0, and S2 scores are confirmatory, and we report every variant we ran; Section~\ref{sec:reliability} states which differences exceed sampling noise.
\item Provincial stratum weights are plot shares, because mapped stratum areas are not public.
\item The cloud climatology uses SCL, whose own overall accuracy is about 80--90\%~\cite{skakun2022cmix}.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Construct.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Plot-scale truth (0.05--1\,ha) is compared with 10\,m to 4.6\,km pixels, and GPS error on small plots adds noise that no estimator can remove.
\item Conformal intervals calibrated on plots cover plot values, not true stocks.
\item GEDI biomass models were calibrated on field plots elsewhere, so E1 is free of our field data but not of field data altogether.
\end{itemize}
\emph{External.} Indonesian plots come from one programme, collected by trained non-foresters; results may not transfer to other forest types or survey teams. Projects in the project-level analysis are region blocks, not registered projects.

\section{Conclusion}
We asked whether open satellite data can replace the field plot in forest carbon MRV, and tested it blind. A sealed right grid was computed step by step to SNI 7724, IPCC, CDM, Verra, and Gold Standard rules with a full uncertainty budget. A left grid saw only coordinates and dates. A hash-chained ledger proves that every prediction was committed before the answer was opened.

Across 476 stock plots, the best blind estimate missed by 45--49\% relative RMSE overall: about 30\% on 1\,ha plots and about 100\% on small Indonesian plots. In Indonesia only 21.5\% of Sentinel-2 acquisitions see a plot clear. Masking at the plot instead of filtering scenes nearly triples the usable data, and a radar-led model loses only two points without optical data. Cloud-aware conformal calibration kept 90\% intervals honest under full cloud, while a metadata confidence score ranked errors in the wrong direction. At project scale, an interval that ignores spatially correlated error covered only 74\% of projects; with field plots as calibration, the satellite halved the plots needed on large-plot datasets. For annual sequestration, no satellite estimator beat ``no change'', and even the field answer was too uncertain to credit without the correlation of permanent plots.

Satellites are a stock-mapping, disturbance, and plot-saving tool, not a replacement for the field plot, and their confidence must be calibrated, not graded. The protocol, the ledger, the cloud-aware intervals, and the extraction runner are reusable. Together they turn a platform's accuracy claim into something a third party can check.
